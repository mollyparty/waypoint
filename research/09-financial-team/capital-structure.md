# Capital Structure: Equity, Vesting and Dilution

> Version-Timestamp: 2026-08-06 14:00:00 UTC-4
>
> **Not legal or tax advice.** Percentages are a recommendation to argue with, not a document to sign. The mechanics of actually issuing these shares, particularly to two minor founders, are in `legal-formation.md` and require counsel.

## 1. What is actually being divided

Not money. Waypoint has no revenue, no product, and roughly nothing in the bank. What is being divided is **the future**, and the honest difficulty is that the three founders are contributing to it in kinds that do not convert into each other.

| Founder | Contributes | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| Asher | The concept, the customer insight, continuous route testing | Origination plus irreplaceable domain access |
| Claudio | The concept, project management, operations, GTM execution | Origination plus sustained coordination |
| Daniel | The entire build, business development, the capital, the only legal capacity | Execution plus risk plus money |

Asher and Claudio originated Waypoint. Daniel is making it exist. Neither of those is worth more in the abstract, and any framework claiming to price them precisely is pretending. What follows is a defensible allocation with its reasoning exposed, which is the most that can honestly be offered.

## 2. Recommendation

**Daniel 40 percent. Claudio 30 percent. Asher 30 percent.** Before the option pool, all subject to vesting.

### Why Daniel takes a premium

He writes all the code, and there is no second builder. He funds the tooling. He is the only person who can legally sign the Apple agreement, the bank documents, or a vendor contract, which means the company's ability to operate at all runs through him. And he carries the personal risk that comes with being the adult whose name is on everything.

Ten points above each of the others is what that is worth here. It is a real premium and it is legible to an investor.

### Why not more

Three reasons, and they compound.

He is **not full-time either**. He runs another business. This is not a founder giving up a salary to work eighty-hour weeks; it is a founder contributing significant part-time effort, which is worth a premium over less effort but not the premium a full-time founder among part-timers would command.

He **did not originate the concept**. The product thesis, the feature set, and the customer insight came from Asher and Claudio. Execution is usually worth more than ideas, and that is exactly why Daniel gets the largest share, but the idea here is not a napkin sketch. It is the accumulated product judgment of a target user, and it is what nine phases of research were built on.

A **majority holder changes the character of a three-founder company**. At 50 percent or above, Daniel can carry any vote alone and the other two become advisory in practice regardless of what the documents say. At 40 percent, any decision requires at least two founders to agree. For a company where two founders are minors relying on the third's legal capacity, that structural balance is worth protecting deliberately.

### Why Claudio and Asher are equal

The obvious objection is that Claudio puts in more hours. That is true: `team-roadmap.md` section 3 models him at 10 hours a week against Asher's 5.

Three arguments against splitting them anyway.

**They co-originated together.** Assigning one of two co-creators a smaller number requires a defensible reason, and "somewhat fewer hours in year one" is not durable enough to justify a permanent difference in ownership. Hours fluctuate. Ownership does not.

**Asher's contribution is the harder one to replace.** Coordination work is valuable and can be done by many people. A committed runner in the target segment, embedded in the team, testing every route honestly and for free, is what most seed-stage consumer teams cannot buy at any price. `../05-product/mvp-scope.md` section 6 states that the real MVP risk is bad routes rather than missing features. Asher is the primary instrument for detecting exactly that.

**A five-point difference between two friends is worth far less than what it costs.** It buys almost nothing economically and creates a permanent, quietly resented ranking. Founder disputes rarely start over a fair split; they start over a split one person privately believes was arbitrary.

If the founders disagree with this reasoning, the honest place to resolve it is the vesting conversation rather than the percentage, because vesting is where actual contribution over time gets reflected.

## 3. The alternatives, argued rather than dismissed

### Alternative A: 34 / 33 / 33, essentially equal

**For it.** Simple, fast, and it treats three genuine founders as three genuine founders. It removes any suggestion that the person doing the building bought his way to a bigger share. For a team of friends who intend to be working together in ten years, the relational value of equality is real and routinely underrated by frameworks that price everything.

**Against it.** It does not describe what is happening. One person is doing the engineering, funding the company, and taking the legal exposure; the other two are part-time in narrower roles. Over three or four years that asymmetry is felt daily, and an equal split converts it into a slow grievance rather than resolving it. Investors also read equal three-way splits at this level of asymmetry as a sign the founders avoided a hard conversation, which raises exactly the question the split was meant to settle.

**Verdict.** Reasonable, and defensible if all three genuinely prefer it after reading this document. But it is the option most likely to be regretted quietly.

### Alternative B: 50 / 25 / 25, Daniel-controlled

**For it.** It matches near-term contribution most literally. Daniel is doing most of the work by any measure. It also gives one person clear decision authority, which can be an advantage when two of the three founders are 15 and coordinating three schedules is genuinely hard.

**Against it.** It overstates the case, because Daniel is part-time too. It creates a controlling founder in a company whose two other founders depend on him for legal capacity already, which concentrates power that is structurally concentrated to begin with. And 25 percent understates the origination contribution to a degree that will not feel fair in three years when the product is recognizably the thing Asher and Claudio described.

**Verdict.** Justifiable if Daniel were full-time and the other two were passive. Neither is true.

### Alternative C: dynamic or milestone-based equity

Allocate a base, then award additional equity as contribution accrues.

**For it.** It handles genuine uncertainty about who will actually do what over three years, which is not a small concern when two founders will make a college decision inside that window.

**Against it.** It requires continuous measurement of contribution, which is the single most reliable way to poison a founding team. It complicates the cap table in ways investors dislike and lawyers charge for. And there is already a standard mechanism that solves the same problem with none of the overhead: **vesting**. Someone who stops contributing stops vesting.

**Verdict.** Not recommended. Vesting is the right tool.

## 4. Vesting

**Four years, one-year cliff, all three founders, no exceptions.** Standard for a Delaware C-corp and expected by any investor.

Nobody should treat this as distrust. It is the mechanism that makes the split in section 2 safe to agree to, because it means the numbers describe a commitment rather than a claim. It protects each founder from the others equally: if any one of the three walks away in month eight, the remaining two are not left carrying a company a third of which belongs to someone who left.

Mechanically the shares are issued in full at formation, subject to the company's right to repurchase unvested shares at the original nominal price if a founder stops providing services. That structure is what the 83(b) election in `legal-formation.md` section 4 attaches to.

### The college-transition trigger

Asher and Claudio are in 10th grade. College applications land in late 2027 and 2028; matriculation in 2029. Availability will change, and pretending it will not is how founding teams end up in an ugly conversation with no framework.

**Recommendation.** Vesting runs normally throughout. Separately, the founders' agreement schedules a **written role review** at each of three moments: when either enters application season, when either matriculates, and at any point a founder's realistic weekly commitment drops by more than half for more than two months.

The review is not automatic forfeiture, which would be punitive toward people who are doing exactly what 15-year-olds are supposed to do. It is a documented conversation with three named outcomes:

1. **Continue as is.** Commitment recovers or the reduction is acceptable. Nothing changes.
2. **Reduced role, reduced vesting.** By written agreement, the founder moves to a smaller defined role and their remaining vesting slows proportionally. Everything already vested stays vested, permanently.
3. **Transition out.** The standard repurchase applies to unvested shares only. Vested shares are kept.

Naming these three outcomes now, while everyone is enthusiastic and nothing is at stake, is worth more than the entire rest of this document. Founder disputes are almost never about the rule. They are about the rule being invented after the situation arose.

### Acceleration

Include **double-trigger acceleration** on change of control: unvested shares accelerate only if the company is acquired *and* the founder is terminated without cause within the following period, typically twelve months. This is standard, investor-acceptable, and protects founders against being fired immediately post-acquisition to reclaim their equity. Single-trigger acceleration is not recommended; acquirers dislike it and it can complicate a deal.

## 5. The option pool

**Recommend 10 percent at formation.**

The usual reason for a pool is hiring employees. Here the reason is sharper: `team-roadmap.md` section 8 identifies four specific triggers for adding a person, and **equity is the only currency available**, because there is no cash for salaries. A GIS contractor, an Android OEM specialist, or a part-time engineer if the AI multiplier disappoints all get paid the same way.

Ten percent is at the lower end of the conventional 10 to 20 percent range, which is right for a company that intends to hire almost nobody before a priced round. It can be expanded later; that expansion dilutes whoever holds equity at the time, which is the normal cost of growing.

Create it at formation rather than at the round. Founders who carve the pool themselves control its size; founders who arrive at a term sheet without one get told what it will be, and the number is usually larger.

## 6. Daniel's cash contribution

Daniel is funding the AI tooling personally today, and will likely cover formation costs before the round closes. Roughly $2,000 to $7,000 over the pre-formation period (`operating-model.md` section 6).

**Do not adjust the equity percentages for this.** A few thousand dollars should not buy points in a company the founders intend to be worth millions; the implied valuation would be absurd in both directions, and it sets a precedent where every future contribution reopens the split.

Two clean options instead:

| Option | Mechanism | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Reimbursement (recommended)** | Log every expense with date and amount. Reimburse from round proceeds at closing | The round closes soon after formation, which is the plan |
| Founder note | Record as an interest-free demand note payable by the company | If the round slips or the amount grows past roughly $10,000 |

Either way, **log it now.** Undocumented founder spend is a recurring source of late-stage awkwardness and a diligence item that looks worse than it is.

## 7. Consideration and pre-formation IP

Each founder pays nominal cash for their shares, typically a fraction of a cent per share across several million shares. Separately, Asher and Claudio contributed the pre-formation concept, product definition and research work product, which should be assigned to the company by explicit written agreement at formation rather than assumed.

This matters for two reasons beyond tidiness. It is part of the consideration supporting their share issuance. And it is the same IP whose assignment is voidable because they are minors, which is why `legal-formation.md` section 2 treats the cure path as the most important item in the whole formation.

## 8. Dilution waterfall

Assumes 40/30/30, a 10 percent pool at formation, a $50,000 friends-and-family SAFE at a $1.5M post-money cap, then a pre-seed and a seed at typical dilution, with the pool topped back to 10 percent at each priced round.

| Stage | Daniel | Claudio | Asher | Pool | F&F | Pre-seed | Seed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Founders only | 40.0% | 30.0% | 30.0% | — | — | — | — |
| 2. After 10% pool | 36.0% | 27.0% | 27.0% | 10.0% | — | — | — |
| 3. After F&F SAFE | 34.8% | 26.1% | 26.1% | 9.7% | 3.3% | — | — |
| 4. After pre-seed (15%) | 28.9% | 21.7% | 21.7% | 10.0% | 2.8% | 15.0% | — |
| 5. After seed (20%) | 22.5% | 16.9% | 16.9% | 10.0% | 2.2% | 11.7% | 20.0% |

**Founders hold roughly 56 percent combined after a seed round.** That is a healthy outcome and comfortably above the level at which investors start worrying that founders are insufficiently motivated.

Two things this table is useful for. It shows that the friends-and-family round is nearly free in dilution terms: 3.3 percent, falling to 2.2 percent by the seed, in exchange for funding the entire path to launch. And it shows where the real dilution lives, which is the priced rounds, not the small one being contemplated now.

### SAFE cap sensitivity

The valuation cap is the only term in a friends-and-family SAFE that meaningfully changes the outcome.

| Cap | $50,000 buys |
|---|---|
| $1.0M | 5.00% |
| **$1.5M (recommended)** | **3.33%** |
| $2.0M | 2.50% |
| $3.0M | 1.67% |

## 9. What to do if the founders disagree

Likely, and healthy. Three suggestions.

**Separate the number from the reasoning.** If someone objects to 40/30/30, ask which specific argument in section 2 they reject. Disagreements about reasoning are resolvable; disagreements about numbers are positional.

**Use vesting for uncertainty about the future, not percentages.** "I am not sure Asher will stay this involved" is a vesting concern, and vesting already handles it.

**Decide once, then stop.** An unresolved equity conversation that reopens every few months does more damage than any particular split. Write it down, sign it, and let the vesting schedule do the ongoing work.

## 10. Assumptions

- [assumption] The entire split, which reflects a judgment about relative contribution that only the three founders can properly make.
- [assumption] Daniel's pre-formation spend at $2,000 to $7,000.
- [assumption] Pre-seed at 15 percent and seed at 20 percent dilution, using the midpoints of the ranges in `fundraising-plan.md` section 5. Actual dilution depends on traction and terms.
- [assumption] Pool topped back to 10 percent at each priced round. Common but not universal, and it is negotiable.
- [verified] Four-year vesting with a one-year cliff, nominal-price repurchase of unvested shares, and 83(b) within 30 days as the standard Delaware founder package.
- [verified] Delaware UTMA custodial ownership as the mechanism for the minor founders' shares (`legal-formation.md` section 3).
